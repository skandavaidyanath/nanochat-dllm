Experiment highlights · phases 1, 2a and 2b

Autoregressive vs masked-diffusion language models on nanochat

Same code, same data, same optimizer. One flag switches the model from next-token prediction to masked diffusion. These are the results from the d12 phase, organised by the question each run was meant to answer.

Shared setup

What was held constant

The two models
AR: causal attention, next-token cross-entropy. MDM: bidirectional attention, a <|mask|> token, masked-diffusion loss with mask rate p drawn uniformly from (0.001, 1) and each masked token weighted by 1/p. Everything else is identical, including the parameter count.
Data and budget
ClimbMix pretraining shards in the same order for both. Batch 524,288 tokens. The 1x budget is 1.32B tokens (12 tokens per parameter, 2,520 steps). SFT uses SmolTalk plus MMLU auxiliary at 3x and GSM8K at 4x.
Optimizer and kernels
Muon on matrices (LR 0.02) with AdamW on embeddings, FlashAttention 3 for both models, logit softcap 15·tanh(x/15).
How loss is reported
Bits per byte (bpb), tokenizer independent, lower is better. The AR number is an exact negative log-likelihood. For the MDM we report two: the ELBO, its training objective and an upper bound, and the left-to-right (L2R) NLL, the exact likelihood when it decodes one token at a time, which is the like-for-like number against AR.
Downstream metrics
CORE: 22 few-shot base-model tasks, each scored as centered accuracy (0 is chance, 1 is perfect), averaged. Noise at this scale is about ±0.005, judged from the LR sweep. ChatCORE: the same idea on ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, MMLU, GSM8K and HumanEval after SFT.
Run names
d12_ar, d12_mdm; _2x and _4x are the MDM at 2 and 4 times the token budget; _lr0.01 and _lr0.04 are the LR sweep; _rep10 is the repeated-data ablation.
Question 1 · Pretraining

Can a diffusion LLM reach the same pretraining validation loss as an AR LLM?

Question / Hypothesis

On identical data, how close does the masked-diffusion model get to the AR model's validation loss, and how much extra data does it need to match it?

Hypothesis. At matched tokens the diffusion model will be clearly worse, because its loss is an upper bound and predicting masked tokens from partial context is a harder problem per token. The gap should shrink with more tokens. Published scaling work puts the diffusion model at roughly 16x the compute to match AR likelihood, so a crossover inside a 4x budget was not expected, but the trend was.

Experiment

  • AR at the 1x budget of 1.32B tokens; MDM at 1x, 2x (2.64B) and 4x (5.28B), each a separate run with the same data order.
  • Muon LR sweep at 1x for both models: 0.01, 0.02 (default), 0.04. This also gives the noise band for CORE.
  • Validation bpb every 250 steps (AR exact; MDM ELBO and L2R). CORE on the final checkpoint, scored for the MDM with left-to-right conditionals.

Findings

No. At d12 the diffusion model does not reach the AR validation loss even with four times the tokens, and the trend says it will not get there by adding tokens alone. Its downstream CORE score, however, catches up.

  • The gap shrinks with diminishing returns. 0.208, then 0.162, then 0.132 bpb. Each doubling buys about 0.64 of the previous gain. A three-point power-law fit puts the MDM asymptote near 0.93 bpb for this model size, above the AR model's 0.847 at 1x.
  • Likelihood and task accuracy diverge. By 4x the MDM's CORE (0.134) is inside the AR noise band (0.140 to 0.147 across the LR sweep) while its likelihood is still 0.13 bpb worse. Loss is not the whole story for this comparison.
  • The task profile is different, not uniformly worse. The MDM leads on algorithmic and reading-comprehension style tasks and trails on commonsense sentence-completion tasks, which are the most next-token shaped.
  • The ELBO is a tight bound. It sits within 0.015 bpb of the L2R NLL throughout every run, so the cheap training-time metric is enough to track progress.
  • Learning rate is not the explanation. The best MDM LR moves it by 0.01 bpb against a gap of 0.2.
  • Training cost is nearly identical. 332K vs 349K tokens per second, a 5% penalty from losing the causal skip in attention.
  • Caveat. The AR model was not trained at 2x or 4x, so this measures how much extra data the MDM needs to match a fixed AR budget, not a compute-matched comparison.
Question 2 · Post-training

Is a diffusion LLM as post-trainable as an AR LLM?

Question / Hypothesis

Given the two 1x base models and the identical supervised fine-tuning recipe, do they gain the same task ability?

Hypothesis. Both should learn the chat format and the multiple-choice and math tasks at similar rates. The diffusion loss is noisier per example, so a somewhat smaller gain was expected, not a different outcome. RL was deliberately deferred until the SFT picture was clear.

Experiment

  • Identical SFT on both base models: SmolTalk conversations plus MMLU auxiliary at 3x and GSM8K at 4x, 934 steps (one epoch), same LR and schedule. For the MDM only assistant tokens are maskable, so the prompt is always visible.
  • Chat evaluation on ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, MMLU, GSM8K and HumanEval. Multiple-choice tasks score the model's distribution over the answer letter at the masked answer slot.
  • Ten diagnostic SFT variants of 200 steps each, scored on the multiple-choice subset (ChatCORE_cat), to isolate the cause of the failure below. Local probes of the raw logits at the answer slot on 200 MMLU questions, and a plain exam-format probe without chat tokens on 298 ARC-Easy questions.

Findings

Not with the AR recipe, and only partly with its own. On the standard mixture the diffusion model learns the chat format but none of the measured tasks. A recipe with three times the multiple-choice share gets it off chance (30 / 30 / 27 against the AR control's 35 / 31 / 30), and neither more training nor a lower learning rate adds anything. The cause is a drowned, half-rate signal, not a representational limit: the frozen base models are equally blank at the answer slot.

  • The high-logit story was a red herring. The MDM's raw letter logits sit at 38 to 46 and are identical across questions, but once the model is uniform over A to D the discriminating gradient is at full strength. Removing the cap confirmed saturation blocks nothing.
  • Three plausible mechanisms were ruled out: loss-weight variance from the 1/p factor, drift in the output layer, and a length leak from packed training.
  • The MDM learns the mapping about half as fast as AR when given only multiple-choice data at a low LR, and essentially not at all within one epoch of the mixture.
  • Working explanation. The gradient at the answer slot itself is the same for both models in expectation, and identical when the minimum mask rate is 1, so the slot is not where they differ. The rest of the batch is. The AR model already fits the long SmolTalk responses well (SFT val bpb 0.356), so their gradients are small and the answer slots are a meaningful share of each update. The diffusion model is far from fitting them (0.624 bpb, and higher per masked token), so their gradients dominate, and Muon's normalised updates follow the dominant direction. The part of the answer-slot signal that every example shares, "a letter goes here", survives because it always points the same way, which is what drives the logits to saturation; the per-question part does not. Removing the competing data, raising the multiple-choice share and lowering the LR are exactly the interventions that helped, which is the evidence for this account.
  • What helps, measured in phase 2a: the multiple-choice share, and only that. Three times the MMLU share at 0.3x LR takes the MDM to 30.1 / 29.7 / 27.4 on ARC-E / ARC-C / MMLU. Two passes with a full warmdown improve its SFT likelihood (0.632 to 0.590 bpb) but not the accuracies; 0.1x LR is worse everywhere; the default share at 0.3x LR stays at chance. The AR model on the same recipe scores 35.0 / 31.3 / 29.9, a little below its default, so the gap shrinks from about 11 / 8 / 6 points to 5 / 2 / 3. GSM8K and HumanEval stay at 0 for the MDM under every recipe. This is the MDM recipe for d24.
  • Why slower, answered by the probes. Neither base model carries a linearly readable answer at the slot (AR 26.0%, MDM 26.1%, chance 25%, even with 20k training questions), so the MDM's features are no worse and SFT builds the skill from nothing in both. The difference is supervision rate: the letter is masked on about half the draws, so it carries loss in 10.2 of 19.5 occurrences per micro-batch against the AR model's 19.5 of 19.5, and the MDM's SmolTalk gradient is larger, so the letter's share of an update is 4.7% against 9.3%. The same half rate matches the phase-1 observation that on multiple-choice-only data the MDM learned at about half the AR rate. In expectation the 1/p weight restores the gradient, but half the examples contribute nothing to a given step and Muon normalises whatever remains.
  • Tested in phase 2b, and it is not the fix. Always masking the answer with weight 1, so the letter gets the same deterministic supervision as the AR loss, scores 30.4 / 26.1 / 25.8 against 30.1 / 29.7 / 27.4 with random masking. The supervision rate was a real difference but not the binding one. What remains between the diffusion model and the AR control on the same recipe looks like a base-model gap, which only the d24 pair can test.
Question 3 · Trade-offs, data

When data is scarce and repeated, which model holds up?

Question / Hypothesis

With the same token budget but only a tenth of the unique data, so that every sequence is seen about ten times, which model degrades less?

Hypothesis. The diffusion model should resist overfitting, because every epoch presents a different random masking of each sequence, whereas next-token targets are identical on every pass. Recent papers report diffusion winning outright in this regime.

Experiment

  • Same 1.32B-token budget and LR schedule for both models, data restricted to the first four training shards (136M tokens), giving about 9.7 epochs.
  • Validation on the fifth shard instead of the usual last one. Both models match their full-data runs within 0.0003 bpb at step 250, so the two validation shards are equivalent.
  • CORE on the final checkpoints.

Findings

The diffusion model does not overfit at ten epochs; the AR model does. At the final checkpoint the diffusion model is better, but an early-stopped AR model still wins, and neither model's downstream score survives the repetition.

  • The MDM lost nothing from a tenth of the data. Its validation curve lies on top of the full-data curve for the whole run, and its training loss is only 0.06 lower.
  • The AR model memorises. Validation bottoms out at 0.967 at five epochs and climbs to 1.101, while training loss falls from 2.80 to 2.16.
  • Who wins depends on the checkpoint. Final checkpoints: MDM by 0.046 bpb. Best checkpoints: AR by 0.088.
  • The likelihood advantage did not transfer to CORE. Both models lose about 0.02 on the repeated subset, the MDM to 0.085. Random masking regularises the loss but did not protect task accuracy at this scale, and the four-shard subset is also a narrower slice of the corpus.
Question 4 · Trade-offs, inference

How much faster is diffusion decoding, and when does it beat AR?

Question / Hypothesis

The promised advantage of diffusion LLMs is parallel decoding. On these models, at what number of tokens per forward pass does the diffusion decoder overtake an AR decoder with a KV cache?

Hypothesis. At one token per pass the diffusion decoder should be slower, since it recomputes the whole block with no cache. It should overtake AR once it commits a few tokens per pass, with the gain growing linearly in tokens per pass.

Experiment

How the diffusion model decodes. It does not write a whole answer in one shot. It starts from a block of 32 mask tokens and runs one forward pass that predicts a distribution for every masked position at once. The sampler commits the most confident predictions, leaves the rest masked, and runs another pass in which the committed tokens are now context. With N steps per block it commits about 32/N tokens per pass: N = 32 is one token per pass, N = 1 writes the whole block from a single pass. Blocks are produced left to right, each conditioned on the finished ones, the semi-autoregressive scheme LLaDA and Dream use. "Tokens per pass" is the parallelism knob. Tokens committed in the same pass are sampled independently of each other given the context, which is where coherence can break down, so the setting trades speed against quality.

A pass and a denoising step are the same thing, one forward pass of the network, so there is no step count multiplied on top of the pass count. The total number of forward passes for a response is steps per block times the number of blocks: a 100-token answer spans four 32-token blocks, so it costs 32 passes at 8 steps per block, 128 at 32 steps per block and 4 at 1 step per block, against about 100 for the AR model. Each diffusion pass runs the network over the whole canvas (prompt, committed tokens and every remaining mask) with no KV cache, while an AR pass with a cache processes one new token, which is why one token per pass comes out slower than AR and the break-even sits at two.

  • scripts/infer_bench_mdm.py on the d12 base models, 256 generated tokens after a 7-token prompt, batch sizes 1, 8 and 32, one H100 PCIe.
  • AR: nanochat's engine with a KV cache. MDM: semi-autoregressive decoding of 32-token blocks with low-confidence remasking, at 32, 16, 8, 4 and 1 steps per block, which is 1, 2, 4, 8 and 32 tokens per pass.

Findings

Diffusion decoding is slower than AR until it commits two tokens per pass, three times faster at four, six times at eight. The quality cost of that parallelism is still unmeasured.

  • Break-even is two tokens per pass at every batch size. Below that the missing KV cache costs a factor of two.
  • The speed-up is close to linear in tokens per pass, slightly sublinear at large batch where the AR decoder uses the GPU better.
  • Accuracy at each setting is still unknown, because the d12 diffusion SFT model has no measurable GSM8K or HumanEval accuracy to trade away. This is the first measurement to make at d24.
  • Absolute numbers are not production numbers. Neither engine uses CUDA graphs and the diffusion engine has no prefix cache. The relative picture is the result.
What the d12 phase leaves open

Open questions

Pretraining

  1. Does the likelihood gap close with scale? The d24 pair (speedrun config, 8xH100) is the next run. At d12 the gap narrowed as training went on; whether a larger model narrows it further is the main question of phase 2.
  2. Is the gap the objective or the starting point? Partly answered in phase 2b: the d12 AR weights continued with the diffusion loss score 2.28 bpb before any training (a random start is 3.17), 1.22 ELBO / 1.12 left-to-right after 250 steps (scratch: 1.44 / 1.45) and 1.20 / 1.16 after 500 (scratch: 1.30 / 1.30), so the AR start is worth most of the scratch run's first thousand steps, and the adapted model stays more left-to-right than a scratch diffusion model. The run's results after step 500 and its CORE score were lost with the pod; the re-run goes on the d24 pod.
  3. What does a compute-matched comparison look like? The AR model should also be trained at 2x and 4x so the crossover question is posed fairly.

Post-training

  1. Is the SFT gap only a speed gap? Answered at d12 in phase 2a: no. The 3x-share recipe lifts the MDM to 30 / 30 / 27 but more steps do not move it further while the AR control sits at 35 / 31 / 30 on the same recipe, and the likelihood gap from pretraining (0.36 vs 0.63 bpb) survives SFT unchanged. Remaining: confirm on the d24 pair, where the base models are stronger.
  2. Why is the MDM slower even on multiple-choice-only data? Largely answered by the phase 2a probes: not a representation gap (both frozen bases are at chance at the slot, the MDM no worse), and the letter carries loss on only about half the draws, which matches the observed half rate. Deterministic masking of short answers was then tested in phase 2b and does not recover the rest, so the remaining gap is attributed to the base model.
  3. Does RL work on the diffusion model? The on-policy REINFORCE path is implemented with an ELBO log-probability surrogate, but its variance and whether it improves GSM8K are untested, and it needs an SFT model with non-zero accuracy first.

Trade-offs

  1. What is the accuracy cost of parallel decoding? GSM8K and HumanEval accuracy against tokens per pass, once a diffusion SFT model has accuracy to measure.
  2. Does the repeated-data advantage ever reach downstream tasks? More seeds, more epochs, and a less narrow subset, since CORE fell for both models while only the AR likelihood suffered.
  3. Infilling. The one capability where a bidirectional model should win outright has not been measured.
  4. Noise. CORE moved by 0.016 across the MDM LR sweep, so differences under about 0.02 need repeated seeds before they count.
